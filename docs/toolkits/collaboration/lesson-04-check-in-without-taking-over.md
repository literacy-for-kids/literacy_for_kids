# Check In Without Taking Over

**Short summary:** Check-ins help groups notice progress without bossing people around.

## Big idea

A check-in is how a group catches problems early — before the deadline, before the resentment, before the surprise. It is not an interrogation, and it is not an excuse to grab someone's part.

## Why it matters

"How is your part going?" and "Why aren't you done?" ask about the same thing, but one invites help and the other assigns blame. Good check-ins ask about progress, stuck points, and support — and give the group a chance to adjust the plan while there is still time.

## Kid-friendly explanation

A dashboard shows how a system is doing — speed, fuel, warning lights — without anyone grabbing the wheel. A group check-in is a dashboard for shared work: everyone can see how it is going, and nobody has to take over to find out.

## Tool: the Three Check-In Questions

> - What is done?
> - What is stuck?
> - What help or change is needed?

## Activity: Helpful or Bossy?

Sort each check-in phrase: does it help the group, or boss people around?

- "What part is done so far?"
- "Why are you so slow?"
- "Do you need anything from me?"
- "I'll just do your part."
- "What is your next step?"
- "Can we change the plan?"

Then talk about how to fix the bossy ones.

## Discussion questions

- How can check-ins help a group?
- What makes a check-in feel bossy?
- How can a group ask for progress kindly?
- When should a group ask an adult for help?

## Try it this week

Use the three check-in questions during one shared task.

## Adult note

Teach check-ins as coordination, not surveillance. Kids may need scripts for checking progress without blame — the difference between "how is it going?" and "why isn't it done?" is a skill, not a personality trait.
