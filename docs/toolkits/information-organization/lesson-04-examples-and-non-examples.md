# Examples and Non-Examples

**Short summary:** Examples and non-examples help show what an idea is — and what it is not.

## Big idea

An example fits the idea. A non-example does not fit. Comparing both helps define the boundary — and non-examples are not "bad examples"; they are useful contrast.

## Why it matters

Lots of ideas are fuzzy at the edges: need vs. want, fact vs. opinion, rule vs. preference. Seeing only examples leaves the boundary blurry. A good non-example — and especially a tricky case near the border — makes the idea click. This works for vocabulary, rules, categories, media claims, legal ideas, health concepts, environmental systems, and decision terms.

## Kid-friendly explanation

If a child only ever sees triangles, they may not know where triangles stop and other shapes begin. Is a squished three-sided shape still a triangle? What about a square? Seeing triangles *and* not-triangles makes the boundary clear.

## Activity: Does It Fit?

Create examples and non-examples for ideas from across the curricula:

- need vs. want
- fact vs. opinion
- rule vs. preference
- source vs. claim
- signal vs. command
- bug vs. feature
- habit vs. one-time action

Bonus: find one tricky case for each pair and discuss what question would settle it.

## Discussion questions

- How can non-examples help?
- Why are tricky cases useful?
- What happens when a category boundary is fuzzy?
- When should you ask for help deciding?

## Try it this week

Pick one new word or idea. Write one example and one non-example.

## Adult note

Non-examples reduce confusion. Use them gently — the point is contrast, not catching kids out.
