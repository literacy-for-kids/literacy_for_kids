# Information Organization Toolkit

Short, practical lessons that help kids sort information, choose categories, label things clearly, use examples and non-examples, take useful notes, make maps and timelines, track sources and evidence, organize digital materials, and find what they need later.

The other Literacy for Kids curricula explain the systems around us — technology, media, money, rules, communities, relationships, the planet, and the body. This toolkit looks at a system kids use every day: how information is arranged so it can be understood and used.

Information organization is how people turn a pile of facts, ideas, notes, files, examples, and sources into something they can find, explain, compare, and use.

Information organization helps people move from:

> "I have a pile of stuff and I don't know what matters."

to:

> "I can sort it, label it, connect it, and find what I need."

## What information organization skills are

Information organization skills are everyday tools for making information usable. They help kids sort information, choose categories, label clearly, compare examples and non-examples, take notes that help later, show relationships with diagrams, put events in order with timelines, track sources, organize digital files, and find information again.

The core ideas are simple:

- A pile is **not a failure;** it is a starting point.
- Organizing is **thinking.**
- Information gets easier to use when it is **sorted, labeled, and connected.**
- Categories are **choices.**
- Labels help **future-you.**
- Examples and non-examples make **boundaries clearer.**
- Notes are **tools, not transcripts.**
- Diagrams, maps, and timelines can **show relationships.**
- Sources need to be **tracked before they disappear.**
- Digital organization **matters too.**
- A useful system **beats a beautiful system nobody uses.**
- Different brains may **need different organization tools.**

This is not "take perfect notes," "write everything down," or "make it pretty." It is practical information organization: how information becomes visible, grouped, labeled, connected, findable, and useful.

## Why information organization belongs in every literacy

Every curriculum asks kids to work with information:

- decisions need options, evidence, outcomes, and tradeoffs
- computer literacy needs files, folders, steps, project materials, and versions
- media literacy needs claims, sources, evidence, and context
- financial literacy needs categories, goals, spending choices, and tradeoffs
- civic literacy needs rules, roles, processes, timelines, and arguments
- legal literacy needs facts, rules, questions, evidence, and agreements
- emotional and social literacy needs signals, patterns, scripts, and reflections
- health literacy needs body systems, habits, observations, and trusted questions
- environmental literacy needs loops, systems, observations, data, and proposals

That is why this toolkit is a **shared layer** rather than another curriculum. The same organization moves appear everywhere: sort, label, group, compare, map, track, and find again.

## How this connects to the other shared toolkits

This is the seventh shared toolkit, and the seven work together:

- **Coping** helps kids stay regulated when information feels overwhelming.
- **Communication** helps kids ask clarifying questions and explain how information is organized.
- **Problem Solving** helps kids decide what information matters for a stuck point.
- **Learning How to Learn** helps kids revisit and use information over time.
- **Executive Function** helps kids make the task of organizing startable and finishable.
- **Collaboration** helps groups organize shared information together.
- **Information Organization** helps kids sort, label, connect, track, and find information.

Together they form a simple sequence:

1. Notice what is happening inside me.
2. Communicate clearly with other people.
3. Define the problem and try a useful next step.
4. Practice in a way that helps me improve.
5. Make tasks visible, startable, and finishable.
6. Work with others toward a shared goal.
7. Sort and connect information so it becomes usable.

You can explore the first six here: the [Coping Skills Toolkit](../coping-skills/intro.md), the [Communication Toolkit](../communication/intro.md), the [Problem Solving Toolkit](../problem-solving/intro.md), the [Learning How to Learn Toolkit](../learning-how-to-learn/intro.md), the [Executive Function Toolkit](../executive-function/intro.md), and the [Collaboration Toolkit](../collaboration/intro.md).

## The Information Organization Loop

This loop appears throughout the toolkit. The full version is great for printables and older learners.

1. What information do I have?
2. What am I trying to use it for?
3. What belongs together?
4. What labels would help future-me?
5. What is an example?
6. What is a non-example?
7. What relationships, order, or patterns can I show?
8. What source or location should I track?
9. How will I find this again?

A shorter kid-facing version works well for quick callouts and younger learners:

> Gather it. Sort it. Label it. Compare examples. Show connections. Track the source. Make it findable.

## Who it is for

These lessons are written for children ages 8–12 and designed to be used by parents, teachers, homeschoolers, clubs, libraries, and small groups. No special training is required. Each lesson is short, no-prep, and built around conversation rather than lecture.

## A note on safety and support

:::caution Everyday organization skills, not a replacement for support
This toolkit teaches everyday information organization skills. It is not therapy, disability evaluation, academic intervention, legal advice, medical advice, or a replacement for trusted adults, educators, caregivers, accommodations, or qualified support. Kids should not be treated as lazy or broken when information feels messy or hard to organize. If a child is consistently overwhelmed, unable to access information, unsafe, or severely distressed, involve a trusted adult and appropriate support. Fictional examples work fine.
:::

## How to use the toolkit

Each lesson takes about 10–20 minutes. Use one lesson on its own, mix it into another curriculum, or work through the full sequence. The printable cards are quick references kids can keep nearby.

### Suggested sequence

1. [Sort Before You Solve](./lesson-01-sort-before-you-solve.md) — turn a pile into useful groups
2. [Categories Help Your Brain](./lesson-02-categories-help-your-brain.md) — choose groups that match the purpose
3. [Labels Help Future-You](./lesson-03-labels-help-future-you.md) — name things so they can be found later
4. [Examples and Non-Examples](./lesson-04-examples-and-non-examples.md) — make idea boundaries clearer
5. [Notes Are Tools, Not Transcripts](./lesson-05-notes-are-tools-not-transcripts.md) — take notes that help you use information later
6. [Maps, Diagrams, and Timelines](./lesson-06-maps-diagrams-and-timelines.md) — show relationships, order, and patterns
7. [Track Sources and Evidence](./lesson-07-track-sources-and-evidence.md) — keep claims, sources, and evidence connected
8. [Build Your Information System](./lesson-08-build-your-information-system.md) — create a small system for sorting, labeling, tracking, and finding

Then keep the [Printable Information Organization Cards](./printables.md) handy for quick reference.

## How each curriculum can use the tools

- **Decision Literacy** — organize options, tradeoffs, reasons, outcomes, and decision journals.
- **Computer Literacy** — organize files, folders, project steps, versions, and digital materials.
- **Media Literacy** — organize claims, sources, evidence, context, and revisions.
- **Financial Literacy** — organize wants, needs, goals, categories, costs, and tradeoffs.
- **Civic Literacy** — organize rules, roles, processes, timelines, arguments, and proposals.
- **Legal Literacy** — organize facts, rules, questions, evidence, agreements, and roles.
- **Emotional & Social Literacy** — organize signals, patterns, scripts, boundaries, and reflections.
- **Health Systems Literacy** — organize body-system ideas, habits, observations, privacy-respecting notes, and trusted questions.
- **Environmental Systems Literacy** — organize loops, maps, observations, data, models, and proposals.

Each of those curricula includes its own short "Information Organization" page and "Information Organization Moment" callouts that link back to the tools here.
