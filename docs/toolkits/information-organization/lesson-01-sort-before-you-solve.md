# Sort Before You Solve

**Short summary:** Before solving, studying, explaining, or deciding, it often helps to sort what you have.

## Big idea

A pile of information can feel overwhelming, but a pile is not a failure — it is a starting point. Sorting means putting things that belong together near each other, so you can see what you have, what is missing, and what matters.

## Why it matters

When everything is mixed together, your brain has to hold the whole mess at once. Sorting is a thinking move, not just a cleanup move: the first sort does not need to be perfect, and you can sort by type, topic, time, importance, source, person, place, or question.

## Kid-friendly explanation

A pile of laundry is harder to use than sorted clothes. You cannot find the sock you need in the mountain — but once shirts, pants, and socks have their own piles, everything gets easier. Information works the same way: when it is mixed together, it is harder to find what you need.

## Tool: the First Sort

> - What information do I have?
> - What groups do I notice?
> - What does not fit yet?
> - What is missing?
> - What group should I look at first?

## Activity: Sort the Pile

Read or copy the supplied cards below. Everything describes an invented mural club; no personal information or outside materials are needed. Paper and a pencil are enough; cutting cards is optional.

| Card | Mixed pile |
|---|---|
| A | The signup sheet lists 8 volunteers. |
| B | A blue mural would look best. |
| C | How many paintbrushes are available? |
| D | An example of a useful label is "blue paint — supplies box." |
| E | Source: mural-club signup sheet, October 6. |
| F | Task: count the paintbrushes before painting day. |
| G | Painting day is October 8, 3–4 p.m. |
| H | Jo coordinates the club; Lee handles supplies. |
| I | The club plans to use the covered courtyard. |
| J | Do we have permission to paint the courtyard wall? |
| K | Task: ask the facilitator to check wall permission before painting. |
| L | A sunset mural would be more cheerful than a blue mural. |

**Steps:** Sort into groups, name the groups, and explain one placement. Mark unanswered questions. Then sort again for a different purpose: "How would you organize this pile to plan painting day?"

**Facilitator notes — useful alternatives:** A first sort by type could place A/I as stated facts, B/L as opinions, C/J as questions, D as an example, E as a source, F/K as tasks, G as date/time, and H as people. G and H also contain factual statements; multiple placements are defensible when explained. A planning sort could group A/E/H as people and records, C/D/F as supplies, G/I as time/place, and J/K as permission; B/L are design ideas. Accept other useful groups. The brush count and permission are still unknown: sorting does not answer them, and the activity does not authorize painting a real wall.

## Discussion questions

- Why can a pile feel overwhelming?
- How does sorting help you think?
- Can there be more than one good way to sort?
- What should you do with something that does not fit?

## Try it this week

Before starting one task with lots of information, sort the pieces into at least three groups.

## Adult note

Do not demand the "right" sort too early. Sorting is exploratory — let kids try a useful first sort, then improve it. The goal is a pile that becomes thinkable, not a perfect filing system.
