# Mistakes Are Data

**Short summary:** Mistakes show what to practice next. They are information, not labels.

## Big idea

A mistake is not "I am bad." A mistake points to a specific part of a skill that needs another look. Used well, a mistake makes you faster at learning, not slower.

## Why it matters

When a mistake feels like proof that you are dumb, it is tempting to hide it or quit. But a hidden mistake cannot teach you anything. When you look at a mistake as a clue, it tells you exactly where the next practice should go.

There is also a difference between making a mistake and repeating the same one without noticing. Noticing is where the learning happens.

## Kid-friendly explanation

Think of a mistake as a pin on a map that says, "Practice here." It is not a label that says "bad at this." It is a marker showing the one spot that needs attention. Once you know where the pin is, you know where to go.

## Tool: Mistake → Clue → Next Practice

> - **Mistake:** What went wrong?
> - **Clue:** What does that mistake show?
> - **Next practice:** What should I practice because of it?

## Activity: Mistake Detective

Look at these sample mistakes. For each one, fill in Mistake / What it shows / Next practice.

- a wrong step in a math problem
- a sentence that was unclear
- a media claim that was misread
- code blocks placed in the wrong order
- forgetting a rule in a game

For example: **Mistake:** added instead of multiplied. **What it shows:** mixed up the operation. **Next practice:** practice spotting the operation word before solving.

## Discussion questions

- Why do mistakes sometimes feel personal?
- What is the difference between a mistake and a label?
- How can a mistake help you improve faster?
- When should you ask for help with a mistake?

## Try it this week

Pick one mistake and finish this sentence: "This mistake taught me ___, so I will practice ___."

## Adult note

Respond to mistakes with curiosity instead of blame. Avoid "careless" as a default explanation — it usually hides the real clue. Ask: "What does this mistake tell us?" That keeps the mistake useful instead of shameful.
