# Use a Checklist

**Short summary:** Checklists reduce memory load. They help your brain stop holding every step at once.

## Big idea

A checklist makes the steps visible so you do not have to keep them all in your head. Forgetting a step does not mean you are careless — it means the steps were invisible.

## Why it matters

When every step lives only in your head, it is easy to drop one, especially when you are tired, rushed, or interrupted. A short, ordered checklist frees your brain to focus on doing instead of remembering.

## Kid-friendly explanation

A checklist is like a backpack for steps. Instead of carrying every step in your hands (where you might drop some), you put them in the backpack and take them out one at a time.

## Tool: Good Checklist Rules

> - Use action words.
> - Keep steps small.
> - Put them in order.
> - Check off what is done.
> - Fix the checklist after using it.

## Activity: Build a Checklist

Create a 5-step checklist for one of these:

- packing a backpack
- saving a computer project
- preparing for a lesson
- cleaning a workspace
- checking a media source
- setting up a science or activity station

## Discussion questions

- Why might a checklist help even if you already know the steps?
- What makes a checklist too hard to use?
- How can checking things off help your brain?
- When should a checklist change?

## Try it this week

Make a 3–5 step checklist for one task you do often.

## Adult note

A checklist is a tool, not a moral contract. If it fails, revise the checklist before blaming the kid — a checklist that is too long or out of order is a design problem.
