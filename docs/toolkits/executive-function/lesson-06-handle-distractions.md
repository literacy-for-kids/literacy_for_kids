# Handle Distractions

**Short summary:** Distractions are attention pulls, not moral failures. You can design around them.

## Big idea

Attention gets pulled by noise, screens, thoughts, hunger, movement needs, stress, boredom, or other people. Noticing the pull is the first step, and the goal is not perfect focus — it is **returning**.

## Why it matters

Getting distracted does not mean you are bad or weak. It means something pulled your attention. Once you notice the pull, you can change the setup: move the distraction, move yourself, change the task, use a timer, write the thought down, or ask for support.

## Kid-friendly explanation

A distraction is like a magnet pulling your attention. Sometimes you can move the magnet farther away (put the tablet in another room), and sometimes you make the task easier to come back to. Either way, you are designing around the pull instead of blaming yourself.

## Tool: Notice / Move / Return

> - **Notice** what is pulling my attention.
> - **Move** the distraction, move myself, or write it down.
> - **Return** to the next tiny step.

## Activity: Distraction Detective

List possible attention pulls in a fictional workspace, then choose one design change for each.

- a tablet nearby
- a noisy room
- a hungry stomach
- confusing instructions
- too many tabs open
- a worried thought
- a sibling talking

## Discussion questions

- What kinds of things pull attention?
- Why is being distracted not the same as being bad?
- What helps you return after your attention gets pulled?
- When might movement actually help focus?

## Try it this week

Notice one attention pull and try one design change.

## Adult note

Avoid "just focus." Help change the environment or the shape of the task. Different kids focus better with different supports — movement, a fidget, quiet, or sound are tools, not misbehavior.
