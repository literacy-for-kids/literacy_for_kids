# Using the Curricula

These curricula are flexible. You can use them in a classroom, at home, or in a community setting by choosing a conversation, a practical activity, or a full weekly module. Preparation varies by the activity.

New here? The [Start Here](./start-here/intro.md) guide covers the fastest ways to begin, including [running your first single lesson](./start-here/use-one-lesson.md).

## Classroom

Use one or more curricula as a short weekly module. They fit naturally into advisory time, social studies, technology class, or a life-skills block. A selected discussion or activity can fit a 10–20 minute slot. Plan several sessions to complete a weekly module and check the actual lesson timing below.

## Homeschool

Integrate a curriculum into social studies, digital citizenship, or practical life-skills work. The discussion-based format works well in small-group or one-on-one settings, and parents do not need to be subject-matter experts to guide the conversations.

## After-school programs

Run lessons as a discussion group, enrichment club, or community learning activity. Choose a short activity for a drop-in session, or plan enough meetings for a weekly module. Gather the listed materials and use an offline adaptation when needed.

## Lesson format

A weekly page is often a module with two guided sessions and independent practice. A short adaptation might select one of these pieces:

1. **Core concept** — introduce the idea through a short reading or prompt (10–20 minutes)
2. **Reinforcement discussion** — revisit the concept with questions and examples (10–20 minutes)
3. **Extension activity** — apply the idea through a hands-on or reflective exercise

## Pacing and preparation

**An activity, a session, and a week are different units of time.** A 10–20 minute entry activity can introduce one idea. Completing the full week means allowing time for its guided teaching, practice, reflection, and any required artifact. Optional extensions add time; new core practices can replace a named activity or use an additional meeting as each lesson explains.

| Example | Published session guidance | Planning implication |
|---|---|---|
| [Media Literacy](https://media.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/curriculum-overview) | Two guided sessions of about 30 minutes, plus 20–30 minutes of independent work | About 80–90 minutes for the standard weekly rhythm, before optional extensions |
| [Computer Literacy, Week 3](https://computer.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/week03-week-3-digital-treasure-chest) | Guided sessions can be 30–40 minutes each, plus about 30 minutes of independent work | About 90–110 minutes for that week's base sessions; prepare apps, folders, and practice files |
| [Decision Literacy](https://decision.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/curriculum-overview) | Two guided sessions of about 30 minutes, plus independent practice | Reserve both guided sessions and the practice time; use the younger route where appropriate |
| [Emotional & Social Literacy](https://emotional.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/curriculum-overview) | Three sessions around 20 minutes, sometimes stretched to 30 | Roughly 60–90 minutes per week; use fictional examples and privacy options |
| Shared Toolkits | Eight short lessons per toolkit, commonly 10–20 minutes per lesson | One toolkit lesson can be a short standalone session; the full toolkit is several sessions |

These are planning examples, not universal timings. Read the actual week, select the route and core activities, and adjust for access needs, group size, and discussion. An 18-week sequence assumes you cover a weekly module in each week. One short discussion per week samples that sequence; it does not necessarily complete all its teaching and practice. A daily 20-minute intensive is similarly a selection of activities unless you schedule the rest.

Before a session:

1. Read the chosen activity and answer/facilitator notes. Check any prior skill it uses.
2. Gather the listed cards, counters, printouts, or other materials.
3. Open and test approved tools, media, files, and devices. Check account/permission requirements before learners arrive; use the paper/offline route when appropriate.
4. Choose an accessible participation option and fictional examples. Do not collect private household, health, financial, or account information for practice.
5. Reserve setup, practice, and reflection time. Some discussions need little preparation; experiments, software activities, fact-check packets, and mock trials need more.

Curriculum reading is free and requires no account. That does not mean every external tool is account-free, every activity needs only the lesson page, or setup takes exactly five minutes.

## Teaching approach

The goal is conversation and exploration, not memorization.

You do not need to be an expert to use these materials. In many cases, the best teaching move is to ask a question, invite examples, and let students explain what they notice.

Because the curricula are modular, you can use a full sequence or choose just the lessons that fit your learners. The curricula can be taught independently, used together, or taught sequentially — whatever works best for your setting.
